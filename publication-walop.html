WALOP-South: a four-camera one-shot imaging polarimeter for the PASIPHAE survey — Paper III: PSF modelling

Paul, I., Deka, K., Ghosh, T., Maharana, S., Ramaprakash, A. N., et al.

Submitted to JATIS, SPIE Digital Library

All publications
Overview

Measuring starlight precisely enough to see the Galaxy's magnetic field

The PASIPHAE survey aims to map the Galactic magnetic field by measuring the polarisation of starlight across large areas of sky, using two purpose-built instruments called WALOPs (Wide-Area Linear Optical Polarimeters). These record all four intensities needed for polarisation measurement simultaneously, on four separate detectors, rather than sequentially — removing systematic errors that would otherwise creep in from changing sky conditions between exposures.

But precise optics alone aren't enough. Reaching PASIPHAE's demanding 0.1% polarimetric accuracy depends entirely on how well stellar flux can be measured and cross-compared across all four channels. This paper builds, validates, and characterises the PSF-based photometry pipeline designed to meet that requirement.

Simulated instrument

Modelling WALOP-South's optics

Realistic PSFs were generated using a Zemax optical model that incorporates the instrument's optical, mechanical, and alignment tolerances — producing 10,000 stellar realisations across the detector to test the pipeline under conditions matching the real instrument.

Method

Modelling light with polar shapelets

01

Why not a simple Gaussian?

WALOP's four polarised beams pass through a shared collimator, a Wollaston prism, wire-grid beam splitters, and dispersion correctors — introducing non-isotropic distortions that a standard Gaussian or Moffat profile simply can't capture.

02

Polar shapelets: a circular-symmetric basis

The PSF is decomposed into polar shapelets — an orthonormal set of basis functions built from Laguerre polynomials — which naturally capture elongation and off-axis aberrations using just a handful of coefficients, and stay well-behaved under Fourier transforms.

03

From bright calibrators to faint targets

A PSF model is built from multiple images of bright calibration stars (m★ = 12), after first solving for each star's precise sub-pixel position, then applied to measure the flux of fainter target stars (m★ = 16) at the survey's actual depth.

Results

Meeting the survey's accuracy requirement

"A hybrid set of just 33 polar shapelet coefficients was enough to model the PSF accurately at every CCD location we tested, while also conserving flux."
  • The 33-coefficient shapelet model reaches a photometric accuracy of 0.15% on faint (m★ = 16) stars — meeting the survey's 0.1% polarimetric accuracy requirement with an exposure time of about 33.5 minutes.
  • Despite its compactness, the model runs efficiently, taking only 1.5–2 seconds per realisation — fast enough for practical use across a wide-field survey.
  • PSF-fitting consistently outperforms traditional aperture photometry, giving consistent, unbiased flux recovery regardless of how distorted the local PSF is — where aperture photometry's performance depends heavily on that distortion.
  • Applied to a simulated multi-source field, the iterative approach performs just as well on realistic, crowded fields as on isolated calibration stars, with no significant systematic structure left in the residuals.
Why it matters

A general-purpose PSF pipeline

Although developed for WALOP-South, the underlying shapelet-based framework isn't instrument-specific — it can be applied to photometry from any imaging system that needs accurate PSF modelling. These results place useful constraints on the signal-to-noise ratios and exposure times needed to meet PASIPHAE's sensitivity goals, laying the photometric groundwork for its eventual tomographic reconstruction of the Galactic magnetic field.
